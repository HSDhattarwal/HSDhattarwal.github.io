Research

How do interactions at the electronic and molecular scale govern the behaviour of a material? My work connects physically faithful atomistic descriptions with the mechanisms behind dielectric response and ionic conduction.

Models & interfaces

  1. 2023 J. Phys. Chem. BDielectric saturation in water from a long-range machine learning model

Physical fidelity beyond the local environment

Heterogeneous systems place demanding constraints on atomistic models: local chemical structure must remain consistent with electrostatic interactions extending across an interface. My research interest is in long-range machine-learning interatomic potentials that retain this physical fidelity, particularly for environments relevant to catalysis and battery interfaces.

Water in an electric field offers a direct test of that description. Our study of dielectric saturation showed that a long-range model predicts water’s nonlinear response even without training on strong-field conditions. As water molecules align with the field, their orientational fluctuations become restricted and the dielectric constant decreases. The result demonstrates transferability of a physically informed description beyond its training conditions.

Mechanisms & materials

  3. 2025 J. Chem. Phys.Electronic fluctuations, cation clustering, and ionic dynamics in molten silver iodide

An electronic route to fast ion transport

Ion conduction in a solid is not simply motion through a fixed lattice. The surrounding electron density can participate in the mechanism. Our simulations of silver iodide (AgI), published in Nature Communications, identified electronic paddle-wheels: the rotation of iodide lone pairs coupled to collective silver-ion diffusion.

This provides an electronic counterpart to the familiar picture of rotating molecular ions assisting transport. Subsequent work connects these electronic motions to the time-dependent friction experienced by silver ions. In molten AgI, iodide polarisation screens interactions between silver ions and promotes clustering. Together, these studies place electronic motion at the heart of a microscopic description of ionic conduction.
