Program Overview

Research Directions

My research program focuses on developing predictive, physics-informed computational frameworks to understand and design functional materials and electrified interfaces for clean energy storage and conversion.

1. Machine Learning Interatomic Potentials with Long-Range Electrostatics

Machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) trained on density functional theory (DFT) data have transformed computational chemistry by enabling ab initio-accuracy molecular dynamics over unprecedented time and length scales. However, the vast majority of standard MLIP architectures (including Behler-Parrinello neural networks, DeepMD, and local message-passing graph neural networks) rely strictly on finite spatial cutoffs (typically 4–6 Å).

In polar liquids, concentrated electrolytes, and ferroelectric materials, this local approximation fundamentally breaks down. Local models cannot capture collective dielectric screening, long-range polarization fluctuations, or response to external electric fields.

To resolve this limitation, we build hybrid architectures that combine equivariant atomic environment descriptors with self-consistent electrostatic models and long-range Ewald summation. Using these models, we investigated the phenomenon of dielectric saturation in liquid water under external electric fields up to the gigavolt-per-meter regime. Our simulations revealed that dielectric saturation arises from a subtle thermodynamic competition between field-induced dipole alignment and the energetic penalty of disrupting the cooperative hydrogen-bonding network.

2. Solid-State Electrolytes & Superionic Conduction

All-solid-state lithium and sodium batteries offer the promise of non-flammability and significantly higher volumetric energy densities than conventional liquid-electrolyte cells. However, achieving rapid ion transport within a crystalline solid requires overcoming rigid framework barriers and high hopping activation energies.

In superionic conductors such as silver iodide (AgI) and complex thiophosphates, we discovered the electronic paddle-wheel mechanism. In molecular crystals, classic paddle-wheel conduction relies on the physical rotation of complex polyatomic anions (e.g., sulphate or borohydride groups) to nudge mobile cations past steric bottlenecks. In atomic solids with monoatomic anions, such mechanical rotation is impossible.

Using ab initio molecular dynamics and maximally localized Wannier functions, we demonstrated that dynamic fluctuations in the local valence electron density—coupled with localized cation-anion polarization—mimic the effect of rotating molecular paddles. These transient electronic fluctuations lower the instantaneous electrostatic barrier for cation hopping, explaining how atomic lattices achieve liquid-like ionic conductivities. We also characterized the temperature-dependent percolation of cation clusters in molten and crystalline AgI, establishing design rules for tailoring framework polarizability in next-generation solid electrolytes.

3. Electrochemical Interfaces & Confined Systems

Electrochemical energy storage devices—including supercapacitors, metal-ion batteries, and water electrolyzers—function through charge transfer and ion reorganization within the electrical double layer at polarized electrode surfaces.

Our work explores the interfacial structure and thermodynamics of advanced electrolyte formulations, including Water-in-Salt Electrolytes (WiSE), room-temperature ionic liquids, and deep eutectic solvents (DES). By introducing hydrophobic ionic liquids into aqueous WiSE systems, we demonstrated that hybrid electrolytes can surpass the conventional 21 mol/kg solubility threshold, restructuring the interfacial double layer at graphite electrodes to dramatically suppress parasitic water decomposition.

Furthermore, under nanoscale confinement inside hydrophobic carbon pores and supramolecular coordination cages, the thermodynamic balance between capillary wetting, drying, and ion desolvation dictates transport rates. In collaborative work with synthetic chemists, we modeled redox-active coordination cages and discovered that metal-center oxidation states can shift encapsulated Brønsted acidity by up to 12 pKa units, opening new avenues for electrochemically switched catalysis and ion selectivity.
